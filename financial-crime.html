01Project overview

I built a transaction-ranking system using 5,078,345 simulated payments from IBM’s HI-Small dataset. The objective was to help a reviewer decide which payments to inspect first, with an explanation for each suggestion.

The project combines SQL analysis, a class-weighted logistic regression model built with NumPy, and a Power BI review report. The model ranks payments for human review; it does not confirm money laundering or make investigation decisions.

02How I built it

  1. Understand the payments. Use SQL to examine payment amounts, formats, currencies, and account activity, then build features from the payment and its sender’s earlier history.
  2. Learn from earlier dates. Train the model on payments before September 8, 2022, validate on September 8, and test on September 9 onward.
  3. Explain the review order. Export the top 10,000 test payments with their scores and feature-contribution explanations to a saved Power BI report.

Modelling decisions

The chronological split tests the model on later payments instead of randomly mixing dates. Historical features use earlier calendar days, and laundering labels are excluded from model inputs; labels are used as training targets and evaluation answers.

Class weighting gives rare labelled payments more influence during training. Explanations show feature contributions to the model score, not causes of wrongdoing. The score is not a calibrated probability of crime.

03Results

At a review capacity of 1,000 payments, the model retrieved 199 labelled positives: 19.9% precision and 12.35% recall. This concentrated positives approximately 107-fold above the test-set prevalence, although 801 reviewed payments were negative and most positives remained outside the shortlist.

The test period contains 863,900 payments, including 1,611 labelled positives (0.1865%). PR-AUC was 0.1016 and ROC-AUC was 0.9330. A score cutoff of 0.5 produced 116,179 false positives; this supports evaluating a capacity-limited ranking rather than treating that cutoff as a practical alert threshold. Full split counts and metrics are in the model report.

04Data & limitations

The 18-day dataset spans 15 currencies and 7 payment formats. Positive-label prevalence rises in later dates. There is no external validation, probability calibration, or real investigator feedback.

This earlier modelling workflow imported amounts as NUMERIC(18,2), which can round higher-precision source values, including Bitcoin. It is separate from the newer banking pipeline, which stores amounts as NUMERIC(24,6). Re-importing and auditing source precision is necessary before asset-level financial analysis; amount comparisons must stay within each currency.

Currency and payment-format associations may reflect the simulator. The enrichment result measures this synthetic time split, not real-bank impact. The Power BI screenshots show a saved report rather than a live monitoring service.

Takeaway & next step. Review capacity makes the model’s usefulness concrete: the question is how many labelled payments a limited shortlist retrieves, alongside the false positives it creates. Next, I would compare the model against simple amount, velocity, and prior-history heuristics at the same review capacities on later data.
